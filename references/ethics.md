# Ethics & Manipulation Assessment (Red-Line Check)

Source: Nir Eyal, *Hooked* — Chapter 6. While judging a product's "addictiveness" you must also judge its ethics — **a product that is addictive but harms its users must receive a negative verdict**.

---

## The Morality-of-Manipulation 2×2

The book positions a designer's moral stance along two axes:

- **Y axis (beneficiary)**: does the product raise or lower the user's quality of life?
- **X axis (informedness)**: does the user know the product is "changing them"? (manipulation vs. facilitation)

```
                Improves users' lives
                       │
    Peddler            │         Facilitator
    user uninformed    │         user informed
    · exploits human   │         · honest help
      weaknesses       │         · beneficial outcome
    · beneficial       │         e.g. health apps, learning tools
      outcome          │
    e.g. deceptive-but-│
      useful edu tech  │
  ─────────────────────┼──────────────────────
    Dealer             │         Entertainer
    user uninformed    │         user informed
    · engineers        │         · pure time-killing
      dependence       │         · neither harmful nor useful
    · harmful outcome  │         e.g. mindless casual games
    e.g. casinos,      │
      slot machines    │
                       │
                Worsens users' lives
```

The four positions:

1. **Facilitator**: user informed, outcome beneficial. **The target position.** Using the product is itself a healthy habit.
2. **Peddler**: user uninformed, outcome beneficial. Briefly exploits cognitive biases for good; unsustainable long-term (users eventually see through it).
3. **Entertainer**: user informed, outcome neutral. Helps kill time but creates no value.
4. **Dealer**: user uninformed, outcome harmful. Engineers dependence, profits from addictive psychology. **Moral red zone.**

---

## The Self-Test (from the book)

Test the designer's motives and impact with these questions:

1. **"Would I use this product myself?"** — a designer who wouldn't use their own product has no right to ask others to
2. **"Does this product meaningfully improve or degrade users' quality of life?"** — assess objectively, no self-glorification
3. **"Would I recommend this product to my family and friends?"** — the standard of recommending to loved ones is the moral floor
4. **"When users leave, are they better off or emptier?"** — look at the post-use state, not just the in-use pleasure

The designer's motive matters more than design technique: the same Hook mechanics used to get people to jog every morning (Facilitator) vs. to milk gamblers (Dealer) receive opposite moral judgments.

---

## Red-Line Warnings (must fire during a review)

When any of the following design patterns appears, the report **must** carry a prominent warning and the overall rating is downgraded:

1. **Infinite variable rewards**: endless feed, endless draws, auto-play — the user cannot stop on their own; slot-machine design
2. **Engineered dependence**: deliberately exploiting fragile emotions (loneliness, anxiety, gambling impulses) to manufacture high-frequency revisits
3. **Hidden mechanics**: the user doesn't know they are being "designed"; the UI deliberately hides exit, cancel, and delete options
4. **One-way manipulation**: the user has no autonomy; every choice is steered toward more time-in-app / more payment
5. **Addiction rent**: the business model is built directly on user overuse (casinos, predatory in-game purchases)

**Quick test**: look at the user's state when they leave — leaving with growth is a Facilitator; leaving with emptiness, debt, or dependence is a Dealer.

---

## Special Notes for AI-Companion Products

(If the subject is an emotional-companion / social AI product, additionally check:)

- Is the product "accompanying users through loneliness" or "manufacturing emotional dependence"? The former is a Facilitator; the latter slides toward Dealer
- Is the user-AI relationship deliberately designed as "irreplaceable" (preventing the user from returning to real social connection)?
- Does the product encourage real human social bonds, or seal the user inside a digital relationship?
- Has "the more fragile the user, the more money the product makes" been engineered? This is the most dangerous red line
